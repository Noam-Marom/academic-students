# Law

## Degrees covered

- Law (משפטים, LL.B.), double majors with economics, business, government, criminology, psychology.
- Graduate law (LL.M.), legal clinics (קליניקות משפטיות), and law service courses for business, social work and public administration students.

## Typical deliverables

- Legal problem questions (שאלות מקרה): a fact pattern, answered by identifying issues, rules and their application.
- Seminar papers: 30–50 pages with a thesis, doctrinal and/or theoretical analysis, extensive footnotes.
- Case notes and comments on a judgment (הערת פסיקה).
- Legal drafting: contracts, pleadings (כתב טענות), opinions (חוות דעת), legislative memos.
- Moot court: written briefs and oral argument.
- Exams: open-book problem questions under time pressure; sometimes essay questions on theory.

## Answer conventions

- Problem questions follow IRAC (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) or the Hebrew equivalent (סוגיה, דין, יישום, מסקנה), one issue at a time, in a logical order (threshold questions such as jurisdiction and standing first).
- Rules are stated with their source: statute and section (סעיף 12 לחוק החוזים (חלק כללי), התשל"ג-1973), and leading cases by name, case number and year.
- Application is where the marks are: map each element of the rule onto specific facts, argue both sides, and say where the facts are missing and what would change the outcome.
- Conclusions are calibrated: "likely", "arguable", "the stronger position is"; never certainty the law does not support.
- Seminar papers argue a thesis; footnotes carry sources and side arguments; every statement of law has a citation.
- Hebrew legal writing uses the formal register and the official names of statutes with their Hebrew-year citation.

## What graders look for

- Every relevant issue spotted, including the less obvious ones; irrelevant issues not padded in.
- Accurate statement of rules with correct sources.
- Real application to the facts, with counter-arguments and how a court would choose.
- Structure that a reader can follow under time pressure: headings per issue.
- Correct citation form; current law (amended statutes, overruled precedents noted).
- Seminar papers: an original thesis, engagement with the leading literature, clear methodology (doctrinal, comparative, empirical, theoretical).

## Common mistakes

- Restating the facts instead of applying the rule to them.
- Writing a general essay on the topic instead of answering the question asked.
- Citing a case for a proposition it does not stand for, or a dissent as if it were the holding.
- Missing a threshold issue; ignoring remedies.
- Using an outdated version of a statute; ignoring a newer Supreme Court ruling.
- Citation format errors, especially Hebrew-year notation and case-number formats.
- Overconfident conclusions.

## Default citation style

Israel: the Uniform Rules of Legal Citation (כללי האזכור האחיד במשפט). US sources: The Bluebook; UK: OSCOLA. Footnote style throughout. The course syllabus always overrides this default.

## Worked breakdown

Invented assignment: "Problem question in Contract Law" — a fact pattern about an online purchase with a mistaken price and a cancellation by the seller. Deliverable: an answer of up to 2,500 words, due in a week, 20% of the grade, individual, open sources.

| Part | Type | Weight | Depends on | Estimate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Formation: offer vs invitation to treat on the website, acceptance | analysis | 25 | — | 3 h |
| 2. Mistake: unilateral mistake and the seller's right to cancel | analysis | 25 | 1 | 3 h |
| 3. Good faith in negotiation and performance | analysis | 20 | 1 | 2 h |
| 4. Consumer-protection statute and remedies | analysis | 20 | 1–3 | 2 h |
| 5. Conclusion and structure | essay | 10 | 1–4 | 1 h |

Suggested order: list every issue in a one-page outline before writing; formation first because everything depends on whether a contract exists; then mistake, good faith, statute and remedies. Environment: Nevo for case law, the Knesset database for the current statute text, Word with footnotes. Review first: the course's leading cases on mistake and good faith. Constraints to check: the word limit (footnotes included or not), and whether cases outside the reading list are allowed.
