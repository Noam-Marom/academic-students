# Humanities

## Degrees covered

- Literature (ספרות): Hebrew, comparative, English, Arabic, French and others; Linguistics (בלשנות).
- History (היסטוריה), Jewish History, Middle Eastern and African History, Art History (the historical side).
- Philosophy (פילוסופיה), Jewish Thought (מחשבת ישראל), Bible Studies (מקרא), Talmud, Jewish Studies (יהדות).
- Archaeology (ארכיאולוגיה), Classics, Religious Studies, Cultural Studies.

## Typical deliverables

- Interpretive essays (חיבור) on a text, period or problem: thesis-driven, 3,000–8,000 words.
- Seminar papers: 20–40 pages, original argument, primary sources at the centre.
- Close-reading exercises and text commentaries (פירוש), translation exercises.
- Source analysis in history: provenance, context, reliability, use.
- Philosophy: argument reconstruction and critique.
- Archaeology: site reports, artefact analysis, field-school diaries, drawings.
- Exams: essay questions and identification of passages or terms.

## Answer conventions

- A humanities essay argues one clear thesis, stated in the introduction; each paragraph advances it with evidence and analysis; the conclusion returns to the thesis and states its implications.
- Evidence comes from primary sources, quoted briefly and then analysed: what the words do, not only what they say.
- Secondary literature is a conversation: position your argument relative to named scholars ("against X's reading, I argue…").
- History: distinguish primary from secondary sources; date and contextualise every source; avoid anachronism.
- Philosophy: reconstruct the argument as premises and conclusion, test each premise, consider the strongest objection, reply.
- Bible and Talmud: cite chapter and verse or tractate and folio (בבלי, ברכות ב ע"א); distinguish peshat from interpretive traditions.
- Archaeology: stratigraphy, context and dating method stated; finds described before interpreted.
- Hebrew academic register; quotations in the original language with a translation when the course requires.

## What graders look for

- A clear, arguable thesis — not a topic and not a summary.
- Close engagement with primary texts; quotations analysed, not decorative.
- Awareness of the scholarly debate and an argument positioned within it.
- Historical and textual context; precise terminology.
- Coherent structure with transitions; paragraphs with one claim each.
- Correct and complete footnotes and bibliography.
- Original thinking at seminar level.

## Common mistakes

- Plot summary or narrative of events instead of argument.
- Long quotations without analysis; quoting secondary sources for what the primary text says.
- Generalisations without evidence ("throughout history people have always…").
- Anachronism and presentism.
- Treating a single interpretation as fact.
- Footnotes that are incomplete (missing page numbers) or inconsistent.
- Philosophy: attacking a weak version of an argument.

## Default citation style

Chicago notes and bibliography (footnotes) in history, Jewish studies and philosophy; MLA in literature departments; archaeology often uses author-date (Harvard). Hebrew sources follow the department's Hebrew citation guide. The course syllabus always overrides this default.

## Worked breakdown

Invented assignment: "Source analysis — a 1920s letter from a Jerusalem community leader" in a first-year history course. Deliverable: 2,000-word analysis, due in 2 weeks, 15% of the grade, individual.

| Part | Type | Weight | Depends on | Estimate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Provenance: author, recipient, date, archive, purpose | analysis | 20 | — | 2 h |
| 2. Context: the political situation and the community's position | essay | 20 | 1 | 3 h |
| 3. Close reading: rhetoric, omissions, intended audience | analysis | 30 | 1 | 3 h |
| 4. Reliability and use: what the source can and cannot tell a historian | analysis | 20 | 2, 3 | 2 h |
| 5. Footnotes and bibliography | other | 10 | 1–4 | 1 h |

Suggested order: read the source three times before any secondary reading; write provenance; read two secondary works for context; then close reading and reliability. Environment: the course's source pack, JSTOR, Zotero with Chicago notes. Review first: the lecture on source criticism. Constraints to check: word limit with or without footnotes, number of required secondary sources.
